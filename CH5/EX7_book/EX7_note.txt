I reused phi_n(x) from 5.6 and wrote each operator as a function acting on phi_j: X*phi(j), X*X*phi(j), phi(j).prime() for D, and two .prime() calls for D^2. 
Then (A)_ij = <phi_i|A phi_j> is a Gauss-Hermite integral as in 5.6, done for i, j = 0..4.

Number of points. After taking out exp(-x^2), the integrand is a polynomial, so I worked out its degree for each operator:
  x:   i+j+1 <= 9   -> 5 points
  x^2: i+j+2 <= 10  -> 6 points
  D:   phi_j' = exp(-x^2/2)(H_j' - x H_j), degree <= 9 -> 5 points
  D^2: degree <= 10 -> 6 points
To check, I compared each matrix with n points against 30 points:

so <phi_i|H phi_j> only has degree i+j <= 8; the degree-10 parts of -D^2/2 and x^2/2 cancel. For the matrices below I used 10 points to be safe.

Everything agrees except the (4,4) element: 4.5 against 2.0. The difference is 2.5. The real matrix product is a sum over all intermediate states:
  (x^2)_44 = sum_k x_4k x_k4 = x_43^2 + x_45^2 = 2 + 2.5 = 4.5.
In the 5x5 matrix the sum stops at k = 4, so the x_45^2 = 5/2 term is lost. Truncating and then multiplying is not the same as multiplying and then 
truncating. Only the last row and column are affected, because x only reaches one step up.

This makes sense. H is diagonal with entries n + 1/2, which are the oscillator energies in these units. 
It has to be diagonal because the phi_n are eigenfunctions of H. The off-diagonal elements of -D^2/2 and x^2/2 cancel exactly, 
and the diagonals add: 0.5*(n+1/2) + 0.5*(n+1/2).

As a check I also built H from the truncated products, -1/2 D*D + 1/2 x*x. Rows 0-3 are the same, but (4,4) comes out 2.0, not 4.5. 
So to get the right H in a finite basis I have to compute x^2 and D^2 directly, not square the truncated x and D.
